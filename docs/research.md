# Northampton Castle — Research Notes

Background research to support the 3D interactive history described in [`design.md`](design.md).
Every claim is tagged with a source key (see [Sources](#sources)). Where sources disagree, the
disagreement is recorded rather than resolved.

## 1. Summary

Northampton Castle was a major Norman castle on the west side of Northampton, just outside the
town's west gate. It was a royal castle for most of the Middle Ages and was used for kings'
visits, great councils and parliaments, and the 1164 trial of Thomas Becket. It was besieged in
1215 and taken by Henry III in 1264. After the Civil War, Charles II had it slighted (1662). It
then decayed into a gaol, orchard and pasture, and the London & North Western Railway demolished
almost all of it in 1879 to build Castle Station. Today a rebuilt postern gate, a length of
earthwork bank east of St Andrew's Road, and buried foundations are what survive. [WIKI][HER-IB][FONC-T]

For a 3D project, the key point is that **no complete plan of the medieval castle survives**.
Reconstruction has to be pieced together from:

- documentary records (royal repair orders, a 1322–3 survey, accounts of Becket's trial);
- post-medieval maps and views (Speed 1610, Tillemans 1719, Noble & Butlin 1746, a 1743 plan,
  *Gentleman's Magazine* 1800, Law 1863);
- antiquarian records of the 1879–80 demolition (Law, Dryden, Sharp);
- excavations in 1961–65 (Alexander) and later work by MOLA (2012–13, 2017 geophysics, 2018
  topographic survey, 2019 test pit). [HER-IB][HER-EX]

## 3. Physical layout (evidence for the 3D model)

### 3.1 Inner bailey

- **Shape:** described as "nearly circular" by early antiquarians; "sub-rectangular" in its
  final form according to Chapman's review. [HER-IB]
- **Size:** about 3.5 acres; one survey gives 486 ft E–W × 324 ft N–S (about 148 m × 99 m). [HER-IB]
- **Bank:** built of ironstone rubble and clay. Where sectioned it was 13 m wide and up to 3.7 m
  high, cut back in the 19th century; originally about 18 m wide × 6 m high. Alexander estimated
  up to 84 ft (about 26 m) wide and at least 20 ft (about 6 m) high before 19th-century cutting.
  The bank was revetted internally at the north-east angle in the 12th or early 13th century to
  make room for buildings. [HER-IB]
- **Ditch / moat:** about 90 ft (27 m) wide × 30 ft (9 m) deep (1961 excavation). Recut as
  recently as the 17th century; still substantially open c.1700 (clay pipe dated 1690–1710);
  filled 16 ft deep in the early 19th century. Older sources give 18 ft at the base. [HER-IB][WIKI]
- **Curtain wall:** stone, set against the earlier earthwork, with plinth courses. Probably
  built between 1153 and 1164. The west wall had two massive bonded buttresses, recorded in 1879
  and probably from the post-1266 rebuild. [HER-IB]
- **Towers:** at least eight. Named ones are *Falkes' Tower* and the *New Tower*, plus six
  smaller towers (1322–3 survey). Speed's 1610 map shows four large towers on the curtain. [HER-IB]
- **South tower ("The Ring"):** circular, with narrow loop lights. Paved for cannon in 1643;
  "a brasse gun on ye Ring" in 1645; later the county prison; destroyed 1879. A bastion of
  20 × 15 ft that served as a prison may be the same structure. [HER-IB]
- **North gate:** principal gateway on the north side, with a causeway across the ditch that
  replaced an earlier drawbridge. 2017 geophysics found possible traces of it under the St
  Andrew's Road site. [HER-IB]
- **Barbican:** earthworks of a barbican north of the inner bailey moat (Law, 1863). [HER-IB]
- **Mound:** a substantial mound about 30 m across in the north-west quarter of the bailey
  (Law, 1863), possibly the remains of the original motte. [HER-IB]
- **Gates:** "two old gates" needed repair in 1323. A south-eastern entrance has been suggested
  for the earliest phase. [HER-IB]

### 3.3 Outer bailey

- About 3.5 acres, defended by a bank, wall and ditch. [HE-C]
- No medieval buildings are recorded there. It probably served as a gathering ground for troops
  and for events such as tournaments. [HE-C]
- A scheduled mound preserves the only surviving part of the outer bailey. [HE-C]
- A **postern** on the west side of the outer bailey had an arch "of semi-Norman character". [HER-P]

### 3.4 What survives today

- **Postern gate:** dismantled by the railway and rebuilt in the station boundary wall near
  Black Lion Hill, faced with reused castle ashlar. It is the only standing fabric. [HER-P][FONC-M]
- **Earthwork:** a 25 m N–S length of inner bailey bank in public open space east of St
  Andrew's Road, with a 5 m section of mortared ironstone wall (a revetment) exposed in the
  1960s. The round mound at its south end is modern. [HER-IB]
- **Buried remains:** walls and wells found in the 1960s are still largely intact under the
  grass. The ditch survives 16 m wide beneath the car park. [FONC-M][HER-IB]
- **Heritage poles** were installed in Castle Park in July 2025 to mark the castle perimeter,
  as part of a storytelling trail. [WIKI]
- **Finds** are held by Northampton Museum (accession 1994.213 for the 1960s material),
  including medieval stylised chess pieces. [HER-EX][HER-IB]

## 5. People and stories

- **Simon de Senlis**, the founder; went on crusade in 1095.
- **Thomas Becket**, tried in the Great Hall in 1164. Contemporary accounts describe him entering
  the hall, waiting in an inner ground-floor room while the king sat in an upper chamber and
  messages passed between them, then escaping at night. [HE-GH]
- **King John**, a frequent visitor who held the Treasury here. In Shakespeare's *King John*
  (Act IV, Scene 3), Prince Arthur dies leaping from the walls. The real Arthur almost certainly
  died in France (c.1203). [WIKI]
- **Falkes de Breauté**, John's mercenary captain and castellan.
- **Henry III and Prince Edward**, who took the castle in 1264.
- **Simon de Montfort the Younger**, captured at the priory breach.
- **Robert Caldecote**, a tenant in 1452 who rented the "herbage within the walls" and fishing
  rights.
- **Edward F. Law and the 1879 antiquarians**, who recorded the castle as it was destroyed.
- **Friends of Northampton Castle (FONC)**, a modern volunteer advocacy group.

## 7. Recommended next research steps

1. Get **Chapman (2021)** in *Northamptonshire Archaeology* and **Chapman, *Northampton Castle:
   A Review of the Evidence***. These are the best modern syntheses.
2. Get high-resolution scans of **Speed (1610)**, **Tillemans (1719)**, **Noble & Butlin
   (1746)**, the **1743 plan**, the ***Gentleman's Magazine* (1800)** plan, and **Law (1863)**.
   Georeference them against modern OS mapping.
3. Ask **MOLA** for the 2017 geophysics and 2018 topographic survey data, which can be used
   directly as terrain input.
4. Ask **Northampton Museum** about photographing finds for in-app 3D or photo objects.
5. Contact **FONC** about their 2012 reconstruction and the 2025 heritage pole trail, for
   collaboration and to avoid contradicting local interpretation.
6. Use *The History of the King's Works* vol. II (pp. 750–53) for the building chronology.
